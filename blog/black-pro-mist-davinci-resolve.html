Black Pro-Mist in DaVinci Resolve: real glass, and how to fake it honestly

By Abramo Benedetti, video editor — last updated 25 August 2026

Watch any A24 film or a modern Netflix drama and you'll notice digital footage that doesn't feel digital: highlights that glow softly, skin that isn't clinically sharp, blacks that stay rich. A lot of that is one piece of glass — the Black Pro-Mist diffusion filter. Here's what it actually does, what it costs, and how to get the look inside DaVinci Resolve, including the Free version.

What a Black Pro-Mist actually does

A Pro-Mist filter is glass with microscopic particles suspended in it. Light from bright sources scatters around those particles before hitting the sensor, which produces two measurable effects: highlights bloom past their edges (a soft halo around windows, practicals, skin speculars) and fine detail is gently softened without looking blurred.

The "Black" version uses dark particles, and that's the trick: the bloom happens, but the veil doesn't lift the shadows — blacks stay dense. The "White" version scatters more light across the whole frame, lifting the shadows into that milky, dreamy look you know from 1980s cinema. In one sentence: White Pro-Mist glows everywhere, Black Pro-Mist glows only where the light is.

What the real glass costs

Physical diffusion is priced like pro gear, and it's per filter, per strength, per size. A single 77 mm Black Pro-Mist for mirrorless lenses typically runs €150–250; the 4×5.65" cinema versions cost several times that, and a matched set of strengths (1/8, 1/4, 1/2, 1) multiplies it again. Shooting with a filter also commits you forever: the diffusion is baked into the negative, and if the director changes their mind in the grade, there is no undo.

That's the honest case for emulation in post: not just the money — the reversibility.

Can post-production emulate it faithfully?

For diffusion, yes — and it's worth being precise about why. The Pro-Mist effect is a spatial light transport: take the energy above a luminance threshold, spread it with a wide-radius falloff, and composite it back over the image (screen blend), while leaving the rest of the tonal range alone. That is exactly what a well-built plugin does. It is also why a LUT can never be a Pro-Mist: a .cube file maps each pixel's color independently — it cannot move light between pixels, so it cannot bloom a highlight.

What genuinely can't be recreated in post: polarization (a polarizer changes what light reaches the sensor) and information lost to clipping. Diffusion, halation, streaks and star patterns are all fair game.

How to stack the mist family in Resolve Free

  1. Install Pulse Color (notarized DMG on macOS, folder copy on Windows) and restart Resolve.
  2. Color page → Effects → OpenFX → Pulse → Pulse Color, drag onto a node.
  3. Pick your camera's log format (or leave Rec.709 for graded/non-log footage).
  4. Toggle Black Pro-Mist and set the grade like you'd order glass: 0.125 = 1/8, 0.25 = 1/4, 0.5 = 1/2, 1 = full.
  5. Stack to taste — a classic interview combo is Black Pro-Mist 1/4 + Glimmerglass 1/4 + a touch of halation. Every effect has its own on/off toggle, so the before/after is one click, forever reversible.

The rest of the drawer

Halation — the warm ring around bright lights that comes from film stock, not glass; the signature "shot on film" tell. Anamorphic streak — the horizontal blue flare of anamorphic lenses. Star filter — cross-etched glass that turns point lights into 4, 6 or 8-point stars. Skin Diffusion — detail softening with no halo, the digital Black Satin. All of them are spatial effects, all of them stack, and none of them can live in a LUT.
