Speed Ramps on the Beat in DaVinci Resolve: Manual vs Automatic

By Abramo Benedetti — August 29, 2026 — 5 min read

A speed ramp — footage diving into slow motion on the drop, or whipping fast between beats — is the single most recognizable move in music-driven editing. Resolve can do it beautifully. It just makes you work for it.

The manual way (any Resolve edition)

  1. Right-click the clip → Retime Controls (Ctrl/Cmd+R).
  2. Add speed points where the change should start and end — on the beat, which you find by ear or by scrubbing the waveform.
  3. Right-click → Retime Curve, then shape the transition so the speed eases in and out instead of snapping.
  4. Set Retime Process → Optical Flow (Clip Attributes or right-click menu). For slow motion below ~50%, push Motion Estimation to Enhanced Better or Speed Warp — otherwise slow-mo stutters.

It works, and for one hero shot it is fine. For a three-minute video with a ramp every few bars, it is an afternoon of repetitive curve-dragging — and every beat you misjudge by a few frames reads as sloppy.

The automatic way (Resolve Studio)

Pulse Studio already knows where every beat is — it detected them to cut your timeline. So it places the ramps for you: it reads the energy and mood of the song, picks the moments that deserve a ramp, and animates the speed change with a proper easing curve, not a linear jump. Slow-motion dives on heavy moments, speed-up whips on energetic ones.

Every generated ramp gets Optical Flow retiming applied automatically — with motion estimation stepped up for deep slow motion — so the frames it invents look like footage, not smearing. The ramps land as real retime data on the clips in your project: you can inspect them, adjust them, or delete any you disagree with.

What a Pulse Studio ramp is, inside Resolve

Open a ramped clip in the Fusion page and you see what was written: a TimeStretcher node between MediaIn and MediaOut, driven by a keyframed SourceTime curve — for every frame of the clip on the timeline, which frame of the source to show. A slow-motion dive is a curve that flattens; a speed-up whip is one that steepens; the easing is the shape of the bend. Pulse Studio writes it through the scripting API by importing a pre-wired Fusion composition, the one route the API connects reliably — verified frame-exact at render.

Three things version 1.0.52 (4 September 2026) changed, after reports from real projects:

  • The curve covers the whole clip. Earlier builds keyframed only the ramp itself (up to 80% of the clip), and Fusion holds the last value past the final keyframe — so the tail of the clip froze. Now the clip keeps the speed it reached.
  • Speed never drops below zero. Elastic and bounce easings overshoot; on a speed curve that meant a clip pausing, or briefly running backwards. They are now floored.
  • The ramp has material to burn. Resolve feeds Fusion the frames beyond the cut — the handles — so a 4x whip on a short clip is a real 4x, provided the source has footage past its in and out points. Trim tight, with no handles, and the ramp stays gentler.
