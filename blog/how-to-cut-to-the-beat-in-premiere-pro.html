How to Cut to the Beat in Premiere Pro (4 Methods, 2026)

By Abramo Benedetti — September 6, 2026 — 7 min read

Let's start with the thing nobody says out loud: Premiere Pro has no native beat detection. There is no button that listens to your track and puts a cut on every kick. Adobe has never shipped one, and as of Premiere Pro 2026 it still hasn't.

So every editor cutting a music video, a reel or a travel montage in Premiere ends up on one of four routes. This guide walks through all four honestly — what each one actually does, what it costs you in time or money, and where each one stops. I make one of them, and I'll tell you exactly where mine is weakest.

Method 1: By hand, with the M key (free, and slower than you think)

The classic. Drop your track on the timeline, play it, and tap M on every beat. Then cut your clips at the markers.

  1. Import the music and put it on an audio track.
  2. Play it back and hit M on each beat you want a cut on.
  3. Snap your clips to the markers and trim each one to fill its slot.

The honest cost. A 3-minute track at 120 BPM has about 360 beats. Even cutting on every other beat, that's ~180 markers to tap and 180 clips to trim. And tapping by ear is imprecise in a way you can't practise away: you don't react to a beat you can hear coming, you predict it — so some markers land early, some late, scattered in both directions. At 25 fps a 40 ms error is already a full frame. You will feel that, and you'll spend a second pass nudging.

When it's the right answer: short pieces, irregular music, or anywhere the cut points are a creative decision rather than a metronome. Hand-marking a 20-second edit is faster than setting up any tool.

Method 2: Premiere's own helpers (they solve a different problem)

Two native features get suggested a lot in forums, and it's worth being precise about what they do:

  • The audio waveform. Zoom in far enough and the transients are visible as spikes. You can place markers on the spikes by eye instead of by ear, which takes the guesswork of Method 1 out of it. It does not find them for you.
  • Remix (in the Essential Sound panel). Remix intelligently re-edits a music track to fit a target duration. It's genuinely clever, and it is not beat cutting — it changes the music to fit your video, not your video to fit the music. If you came looking for auto-cut and someone pointed you at Remix, that's why it didn't do what you expected.

When it's the right answer: the waveform trick is a real upgrade over tapping by ear and costs nothing. Use it. Remix is for when your edit is locked and your track is the wrong length.

Method 3: A plugin that runs inside Premiere

The established option here is BeatEdit by Mamoworld — the original beat-detection plugin in this space, around for years, with a serious reputation among Premiere editors. It analyses your track and places markers on the Premiere timeline for you, inside Premiere, as part of your normal session.

That "inside Premiere" part is the real advantage, and I want to be straight about it: a tool that lives in your host application will always beat a file you have to import. No round trip, no second app, no export step. If you cut in Premiere every day and beat-syncing is a regular part of your work, a native plugin is the workflow that fits.

The honest cost: it's a paid plugin, licensed per host application. Check Mamoworld's site for current pricing — I'm not going to quote a number here that might be out of date by the time you read this.

Method 4: Detect the beats elsewhere, import a finished timeline

The fourth route inverts the problem. Instead of a plugin reaching into Premiere, a separate app analyses the music, builds the whole cut, and hands Premiere a timeline file to import.

This is what Pulse Edit does. I built it, so treat this section as what it is — but the limits below are the real ones, not marketing softeners.

It detects the beats in your track, picks cut points from a pattern you choose, assembles your clips against them, and writes out a Premiere Pro XML containing the cuts, the music track and the beat markers. You import the XML; the sequence appears already cut.

Where this is weak, stated plainly.

  • The Premiere and Final Cut exports are in beta. The files are written to spec — the FCPXML validates against Apple's DTD — but we have not opened them inside Premiere Pro or Final Cut Pro ourselves. Not once. If you try it and it breaks, that is a real possibility and I want to hear about it: support@pulseedit.com.
  • Speed ramps and zooms do not cross over. Those are DaVinci Resolve Studio only. What travels to Premiere is cuts, music and markers.
  • It's an import step, not a panel inside Premiere. If you want the tool to live in your host app, Method 3 is the honest recommendation.

Where it earns its place: the export is included in the same €13/month licence as the DaVinci Resolve version — there is no separate per-host purchase. And it doesn't stop at markers: it assembles the cut, rather than telling you where the beats are and leaving the 180 trims to you.

Common questions

Does Premiere Pro have beat detection?

No. Premiere Pro has no native beat detection as of 2026. You can place markers by hand or read the transients off the audio waveform, but nothing in Premiere finds the beats for you. Beat detection comes from a third-party plugin or from an external app that writes a timeline you import.

Can I cut to the beat in Premiere Pro for free?

Yes — Methods 1 and 2 cost nothing and are entirely native. Zooming into the waveform and marking the visible transients is the more accurate of the two, because you are reading where the beat actually is instead of guessing when it will arrive.

Does Pulse Edit work with Premiere Pro?

Pulse Edit exports a Premiere Pro XML containing the cuts, the music and the beat markers, and this export is in beta: the file is written to spec, but we have not yet opened one inside Premiere Pro ourselves. Speed ramps and zooms stay DaVinci Resolve Studio only. Pulse Edit does not run inside Premiere — it produces a timeline you import. Details on the Premiere Pro page.

What's the difference between beat markers and beat-synced editing?

Markers tell you where the beats are; you still cut and trim every clip yourself. Beat-synced editing places the clips against those beats for you. On a 3-minute track that difference is roughly 180 manual trims. It's the main thing to be clear about when comparing tools, because plugins in this space do one, the other, or both.

Which is better, cutting in Premiere or in DaVinci Resolve?

For beat-syncing specifically, Resolve currently has more automation available to it: speed ramps and animated zooms are built through the DaVinci Resolve Studio scripting API and live only in Pulse Studio — a different tool in the same licence, which needs the paid DaVinci Resolve Studio. The Premiere export does not carry them. If you already edit in Premiere, that alone isn't a reason to switch; it's a reason to pick your beat-sync route from the four above.
